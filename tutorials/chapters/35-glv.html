Level 3 · Bitcoiner · chapter 35

35 The GLV endomorphism

Level 3 · Bitcoiner. secp256k1 has a hidden symmetry: multiplying x by a cube root of unity β maps the curve to itself, and on the group that is multiplication by a fixed scalar λ. You will see it as three points on one ring, type it by hand, and list the six keys it relates. Read first: 31 secp256k1 in the panel.

β and λ

Because p ≡ 1 (mod 3), F_p has two nontrivial cube roots of unity: numbers β ≠ 1 with β³ = 1. Since a = 0,

(βx)³ + 7 = β³x³ + 7 = x³ + 7,

so if (x, y) is on the curve, so is (βx, y): the map (x, y) ↦ (βx, y) is an automorphism of the curve. On the cyclic group of order n it must be multiplication by some fixed number, and that number is λ, a cube root of unity mod n. The pair the visualizer (and libsecp256k1) uses:

  • β = 7ae96a2b657c07106e64479eac3434e99cf0497512f58995c1396c28719501ee (mod p), β² = 851695d49a83f8ef919bb86153cbcb16630fb68aed0a766a3ec693d68e6afa40;
  • λ = 5363ad4cc05c30e0a5261c028812645a122e22ea20816678df02967c1b23bd72 (mod n), λ² = ac9c52b33fa3cf1f5ad9e3fd77ed9ba4a880b9fc8ec739c2e0cfc810b51283ce;
  • 1 + β + β² ≡ 0 (mod p) and 1 + λ + λ² ≡ 0 (mod n).

So λ·(x, y) = (βx, y): one field multiplication instead of a scalar multiplication. libsecp256k1 uses this (the Gallant–Lambert–Vanstone method) to split a 256-bit scalar into two halves of about 128 bits and multiply faster.

Example 35.1 · G, λG and λ²G share a y

The preset “secp256k1: G and λG (GLV, same y)”, from the top (key 2), with the β-orbit switch on.

Open this view: G and λG from the top

Do it yourself

  1. In Curve, pick the preset secp256k1: G and λG (GLV, same y).
  2. Press 2 for the top view.
  3. In the 3D view, hover P₂; its card stays until you hover another point.
  4. Read the Relations section in the panel.

Look at:

  • P₁ = G and P₂ = λG on one ring around the hole (the same y means the same parallel); the small rings mark the third point, λ²G, on the same ring;
  • Relations: same y “yes, same parallel ring” and β link “P₂ = λ·P₁ (x₂ = β·x₁)”;
  • the message under P₂: λ·G, with λ shortened to its first and last digits; its full value is given above.

Why: λG = (βx, y) and λ²G = (β²x, y). Their x values are bcace2e99da01887ab0102b696902325872844067f15e98da7bba04400b88fcb and c994b69768832bcbff5e9ab39ae8d1d3763bbf1e531bed98fe51de5ee84f50fb; their compressed keys are those x with the prefix 02, like G, because the y is the same.

Do it yourself

  1. In Curve, pick the preset secp256k1: G and λG (GLV, same y).
  2. Type (beta * 0x79be667ef9dcbbac55a06295ce870b07029bfcdb2dce28d959f2815b16f81798 % p, 0x483ada7726a3c4655da4fbfc0e1108a8fd17b448a68554199c47d08ffb10d4b8) in P₂.
  3. Read the Relations section in the panel.

Look at: “(x, y) pair, on the curve”, and the same β link as in Example 35.1: the point you typed from coordinates is recognised as λ·G.

Why: the visualizer checks for every pair with the same y whether x₂ = βx₁ or β²x₁. The β-orbit switch draws the two siblings of any point, so you can see the relation on points you did not construct.

Do it yourself

  1. In Curve, pick the preset secp256k1: G and λG (GLV, same y).
  2. Type -λ^2G in P₁ and -G in P₂.
  3. Press 2 for the top view.
  4. Read the Relations section in the panel.

Look at: −λ²G and −G share a y (the mirror image of G’s ring), and Relations links them by λ again. The table types all six: G, λG, λ²G and their negatives; each row gives the private key mod n and the public key.

Why: for any key k, the six keys ±k, ±λk, ±λ²k (mod n) have public keys that share an x up to β (three x values) and a y up to sign (two y values). Knowing one public key gives the other five for free. idleCore’s scalar_orbit and point_orbit compute exactly these six, and the build checks them against the visualizer.

Example 35.4 · On the sphere: one latitude

Open this view: G and λG on the sphere

Do it yourself

  1. In Curve, pick the preset secp256k1: G and λG (GLV, same y).
  2. In Shape, pick Sphere (or press S until it shows).
  3. Press 2 for the top view.
  4. In the 3D view, hover P₂; its card stays until you hover another point.

Look at: from the top, the three points of the orbit on one circle of latitude.

Why: on the sphere y sets the latitude, so a shared y is a shared latitude; the three x values give three different longitudes.

Example 35.5 · On F₂₂₃: the three points with y = 0

Toy curves with a = 0 and p ≡ 1 (mod 3) have the same structure. On F₂₂₃, β = 183 and β² = 39 (no λ is named there, since the group is not cyclic).

Open this view: (6, 0), (11, 0) and the β-orbit

Do it yourself

  1. In Curve, pick the preset y² = x³ + 7 over F₂₂₃, Programming Bitcoin.
  2. Type (6, 0) in P₁ and (11, 0) in P₂.
  3. In Display, turn on β-orbit (B).
  4. In Display, turn off Chord construction (K).
  5. Press 2 for the top view.
  6. Read the Relations section in the panel.

Look at: the β-orbit of (6, 0) is (206, 0) and (11, 0): 183 × 6 = 1098 ≡ 206 and 39 × 6 = 234 ≡ 11. Relations writes the link without λ: “x₂ = β²·x₁, same y”.

Why: the three roots of x³ + 7 = 0 are x, βx and β²x, so the three points of order 2 (23) form one β-orbit. The same holds for every y: the x values sharing a y come in threes, which is why the F₁₀₀₀₉ cloud has three dots or none in every row (12).

Try it yourself

  • Type a made-up key into P₁ as 0x, its 64 hex digits and G, and the same with λ* in front into P₂: Relations shows the β link.
  • Turn the β-orbit on (key B) in the default view: G and −G each get their two siblings.
  • On P-256, the β-orbit switch is greyed out. Why? (39)

Recap

  • With a = 0 and p ≡ 1 (mod 3), (x, y) ↦ (βx, y) maps the curve to itself; on secp256k1 it is multiplication by λ.
  • G, λG and λ²G share y (one parallel ring, one latitude); with the negatives, every key comes with five siblings.
  • Relations reports the β link; the β-orbit switch (B) draws the siblings.
